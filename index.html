Does it work? And what could it cost you?

Twelve things men try in order to look better, sorted from worth doing to dangerous.

EvidenceHow good the research is. Strong means repeated controlled trials in people.

RiskHow badly it can go wrong, and whether the damage can be undone.

Worth doing

Good evidence, and little that can go wrong.

  • Daily sunscreen (SPF 30+)

    Strong evidenceLow risk

    Do it. The cheapest thing on this list that is proven to slow skin ageing.

  • Lifting weights and eating enough protein

    Strong evidenceLow risk

    Do it. The biggest visible change most men can make.

  • Creatine monohydrate

    Strong evidenceLow risk

    One of the few supplements that does what it says.

  • Peroxide teeth whitening

    Strong evidenceLow risk

    Works on natural teeth. Follow the instructions.

Works, with trade-offs

Good evidence, but side effects or a prescription are involved. Read the page before you start.

  • Topical retinoids (tretinoin, adapalene)

    Strong evidenceMedium risk

    Works for acne and sun-aged skin. Start slowly.

  • Topical minoxidil

    Strong evidenceMedium risk

    Works for male pattern hair loss, but only for as long as you keep using it.

  • Finasteride

    Strong evidenceMedium risk

    The best-studied pill for male pattern hair loss. A decision to make with a doctor.

Unproven

Little or no good evidence. The usual cost is time, money or false hope.

  • Home microneedling for hair loss

    Mixed evidenceMedium risk

    Promising, not settled. Clinic-grade results do not transfer automatically to a roller at home.

  • Mewing (tongue posture)

    Weak evidenceLow risk

    Harmless, but no good evidence it reshapes an adult face.

  • Hard chewing and jaw trainers

    Weak evidenceMedium risk

    Little to gain, and a real chance of jaw pain.

Dangerous

The harm is real and can be permanent.

  • Steroids and SARMs for looks

    Strong evidenceHigh risk

    They build muscle. The price can be your heart, hormones and fertility.

  • Bonesmashing

    No evidenceHigh risk

    Do not do this.

Why two grades instead of one score

Because they come apart. Steroids build muscle and can wreck your heart. Mewing is harmless and does very little. One score would hide both facts.

How the grades were decided

"Strong" means repeated controlled trials in people. "Mixed" means a few small or inconsistent trials. "Weak" and "none" mean the claim rests on theory, anecdotes or before-and-after photos. Each practice has its own page with a named study where a good one exists, that study's limit, and what new evidence would change the grade.

Refreshed is not the same as reviewed

A script re-runs each PubMed search every week and updates the paper count on each page. That number shows how much research exists, not how good it is. The grades were written once, on 11 October 2026, and are not re-checked automatically. No doctor has reviewed them.

How this was built

Made by Soham Umap with Claude Code as a demo for looksmaxxing.guide, in a 45-minute test window. The AI wrote the code and the first draft of every entry. My calls: keep evidence and risk as two separate grades, pull live numbers from a free public source instead of a paid scraper, and name a study only where its PubMed record was confirmed. The eight named studies were checked to exist and match their titles. They were not all read in full.

The layout was simplified later the same day, after the test was submitted, because the first version was cluttered. The entries and grades did not change.
